What a Protective Driver Learns First

Armored vehicle work starts with physics and cones, not with speed. Here is the first block of the course and why it is built that way.

6 min read · MAST Solutions · Houston, Texas

Archival note: this lesson plan was first written in May 2002 and revised as version 1.00 in July 2011. The physics has not changed. Vehicles, braking systems and client-specific procedures have, and none of the latter appear here.

Home / MAST Solutions / Articles / Armored Vehicle Operator

People assume protective driving training begins with speed. It begins with four traffic cones and a conversation about physics, and there is a reason the order is that way around.

What follows is the first block of our armored vehicle operator lesson plan: what it covers, why each piece is in it, and what an instructor is actually watching for.

Safety Comes Before Anything Moves

Before a student touches a vehicle, the instructor completes a vehicle safety inspection and a track safety inspection. The cars have to be mechanically acceptable for what is about to be asked of them, and that is checked, not assumed.

The rules on the driving course are short and they are not negotiable:

  • No horseplay.
  • Seat belts on at all times in an exercise vehicle.
  • Helmets on at all times during vehicle movement in the exercise area.
  • Drivers maintain vehicle order through an exercise.
  • Radio discipline, with instructor staff giving directions to drivers.
  • Nobody exceeds the speed the instructor has set.
  • Students not driving stay well clear of the exercise area.

A driving course is the one training environment where a moment of enthusiasm produces a multi-thousand-pound consequence. The rules exist so the instructor can spend attention on teaching rather than on watching for something stupid.

Exercise One: The Slalom

Every good driving course begins with a slalom, and not because it is a useful maneuver on the road. It is a diagnostic.

Four cones, evenly spaced at distances set by the vehicle, the space available, and the lateral forces the instructor wants to produce. The student enters with the right side of the vehicle positioned to the left of the first cone, establishes speed early, and holds the same speed in and out.

In roughly ninety seconds it tells the instructor four things:

  • The student feels lateral force in the seat of their pants, most of them for the first time.
  • The instructor can assess whether they can control those forces.
  • The instructor can assess hand-eye coordination and distance perception.
  • The instructor gets an honest read on the class's driving skill before anything harder is attempted.

The third cone

The problem students hit is almost always the third cone. It is so consistent that we teach it in advance. Hitting the third cone is caused by one of three things, and it is worth knowing which is yours:

  • A late entry at the first cone, which puts you behind for the rest of the run.
  • Failing to look through the course.
  • Lack of smoothness on the controls.

The second one is the important one, and it generalizes far beyond driving. Use your eyes. Look through the course. Do not stare at the cone in front of you. A driver who fixates on the nearest obstacle arrives at every subsequent one unprepared, and by the third they have run out of margin.

“Look through the course. The driver who stares at the nearest cone is already late for the next one.”

And the standing instruction if it comes apart: if you get in trouble during the exercise, hit the brakes. Not a heroic correction. Brakes.

The Physics, Because the Vehicle Obeys It

After the slalom, the class goes back to the classroom, and how far into this we go depends on the group in front of us. The limits of a vehicle come from simple formulas based on speed, gravity, time and distance, and a driver who understands them stops being surprised by them.

Converting speed into distance

To convert miles per hour to feet per second, multiply by 1.47. That number is not arbitrary: a mile is 5,280 feet, divided by the seconds in an hour, which gives 1.47. In your head, multiply by 1.5. Going the other way, divide by 1.47, or by 1.5 for a rough figure.

This is the single most useful piece of arithmetic on the course, because it converts an abstraction into a distance. At thirty miles per hour you are covering roughly forty-five feet every second. Whatever your reaction time is, multiply it by that.

Radius

You create a radius every time you drive around an object or a corner, and through every slalom and accident-avoidance run. A radius is a part of a circle, an arc, and also the path the front wheels take when the wheel is turned. The more the wheel is turned, the smaller the radius.

The radius determines the forces on the vehicle and on the body in it. If the forces applied exceed what the vehicle can accept, it leaves the track. In the slalom this shows up directly: the further apart the cones, the bigger the radius, and the bigger the radius, the lower the g-forces on the vehicle.

Which is why cone spacing is an instructor's control, not a decoration. It is how the difficulty gets dialed to the class.

Exercise Two: Brake and Turn

The purpose is to demonstrate something most drivers do not believe until they have done it: you can brake and turn at the same time, miss the obstacle, and keep directional control.

ABS technique

Drive into the starting point at the designated speed, bring the vehicle to a complete stop with a hard application of the brake pedal, and stay inside the boundaries of the course, steering as required to do so.

The technique in practice:

  • Establish speed early.
  • Maintain speed to the entry point.
  • Turn and brake at the same time.
  • Brake hard and hold the pedal.
  • Come to a complete stop.

The tires will make a skidding noise and the vehicle will vibrate. Students back off the pedal when that happens, every time, on the first run. Being told in advance that it is going to feel and sound wrong is most of the fix. The objective is control of the vehicle, not a tidy stop.

Threshold braking

Whether this gets taught depends on the vehicles the students actually operate. It is the manual version of what ABS does for you, and it is difficult.

A sliding tire is one hundred percent locked and has no slip. A tire delivers its greatest braking capability at about eighty percent of slip. Threshold braking is holding roughly twenty percent less force than would lock the tire, which means finding that eighty percent point and then staying on it while the inertia of the vehicle changes as it slows. Finding it is hard. Holding it is harder.

What Comes After the First Block

The slalom and brake-and-turn are lessons one and two of a longer progression. The full operator syllabus works through high-speed turns, accident avoidance, backing in a straight line, the J turn, motorcade follow-on driving, and vehicle barricade breaching.

Each of those is a harder application of the same two things established on day one: the vehicle obeys physics, and the driver's eyes go where the vehicle is going next. Everything else is repetition and a coach on the radio.

Protective driving sits alongside the rest of our vehicle work rather than replacing it. Vehicular Tactics covers working in and around the vehicle, and Motorcade covers moving as a formation. This block covers the part where one person has to control one vehicle at the edge of what it will do.

Common Questions

Do I need an armored vehicle to take a protective driving course?

No. The fundamentals block is taught in ordinary vehicles on a driving course, and that is deliberate. Weight and armor change the numbers but not the principles, and a driver who cannot control a normal car smoothly will not control a heavier one.

What is the difference between ABS braking and threshold braking?

With ABS you brake hard and hold the pedal, and the system manages the wheels for you while you steer. Threshold braking is the manual version: holding the brakes just short of lockup, around eighty percent of slip, where a tire brakes best. It is genuinely difficult, because that point moves as the vehicle slows.

Why does the course start with cones instead of speed?

Because the slalom tells the instructor almost everything within about ninety seconds: your hand-eye coordination, your distance perception, your smoothness on the controls, and whether you look ahead or fixate. Speed added to a driver who has not been assessed is not training, it is a liability.

Is this the same as the Vehicular Tactics course?

No. Vehicular Tactics covers working in and around a vehicle. This is operator training: controlling the vehicle itself at the limits of its handling. They are complementary and the driving side usually comes first.
